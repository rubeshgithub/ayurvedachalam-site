# Are Ayurvedic Herbs Safe?

*By Maheswari Vedhachalam, BAMS (Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery) · Ayurveda Practitioner, Calgary · Reviewed [date set in CMS]*

Some are, and some are not, and the difference is not the herb. It is who made the product, whether Health Canada has licensed it, and whether anyone checked what is in the bottle. Licensed Ayurvedic herbal products sold in Canada carry an eight-digit Natural Product Number (NPN) and have been reviewed for safety, quality and the claims on the label. Unlicensed products, especially ones brought from abroad or bought from a clinic's back room, are where the documented harm has come from. This article explains both halves, so that you can tell them apart in a shop or at a practitioner's desk, including mine.

## Where the "heavy metals" story comes from

The concern is real and has a specific source. Classical Ayurveda includes a branch, rasa shastra, that uses purified metals and minerals in preparations called bhasmas. Done to the letter of the tradition, the metals are processed repeatedly; done badly, or with poor manufacturing, lead, mercury and arsenic remain in the finished product at harmful levels.

Health Canada has warned about this more than once. In 2008 it reminded consumers that some unapproved Ayurvedic products contain high levels of heavy metals, citing a British Columbia Medical Journal report of a man who had been taking a product bought in India for years and was found to have very high levels of lead, mercury and arsenic. In November 2021 it warned the public about products sold by an Ayurvedic clinic in Toronto after Public Health Ontario linked a lead-poisoning case to them. When Health Canada tested products seized from that clinic, 14 of 15 samples contained high levels of arsenic, mercury or lead, and three also contained prescription drugs that were not on the label.

Two things stand out in those cases. The products were unlicensed. And they were sold by people presenting themselves as Ayurvedic practitioners. That is why "my practitioner recommended it" is not, on its own, a safety check.

## What an NPN tells you, and what it does not

Every natural health product sold in Canada needs a product licence from Health Canada before it can be marketed. When a licence is granted, the label carries an eight-digit number preceded by the letters NPN (homeopathic products use DIN-HM instead). To get that number, the company has to tell Health Canada what the medicinal ingredients are, where they come from, their strength, the non-medicinal ingredients and the intended use, and the product must be made in a site that holds a site licence, which is Health Canada's check that good manufacturing practices are followed. Health Canada says the number "assures consumers that the product has been reviewed and approved."

You can look any NPN up in the Licensed Natural Health Products Database, which lists the licence holder, ingredients, dose form, intended use and risk information. If the number on the label is not in the database, or there is no number, treat the product as unlicensed.

What the NPN does not tell you is whether the product is right for you. Licensed herbs still interact with prescription medicines, still have people who should not take them, and are still sold with marketing that outruns the evidence. The NPN is the floor, not the ceiling.

## Which Ayurvedic herbs are commonly licensed in Canada?

Many single herbs that Ayurveda uses have licensed products on Canadian shelves: ashwagandha, turmeric and curcumin, triphala, brahmi (Bacopa), tulsi (holy basil), amla, shatavari, guggul, neem and ginger among them. Health Canada publishes monographs for several of these that set out permitted claims, doses and cautions; a licensed product's label follows its monograph.

What you will not find licensed, and what this practice never recommends, are bhasmas, rasa preparations, or any metal- or mineral-based product. Nor do we recommend anything brought in a suitcase from India, however trusted the family pharmacy there. The product may be fine. Nobody in Canada has checked, and the two Health Canada warnings above are what "nobody checked" looks like.

## How to check a product in two minutes

1. **Find the NPN on the label.** Eight digits after the letters NPN. No number means no licence.
2. **Look it up** in Health Canada's Licensed Natural Health Products Database and confirm the product name and company match.
3. **Read the risk information** in the database entry: who should not take it, and what it interacts with.
4. **Tell your physician and pharmacist** what you are taking. Ashwagandha, turmeric, guggul and triphala each have known interactions or cautions; your pharmacist can check them against your prescriptions in a minute.
5. **Be suspicious of fast results.** A herbal product that works like a drug may contain one. Three of the Toronto samples did.

I do not sell products, I do not dose herbs for a diagnosed medical condition, I do not give herbal doses for children or in pregnancy, and I will tell you plainly when something you have been taking should go in the bin. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is ashwagandha safe in Canada?**
Licensed ashwagandha products carry an NPN and follow Health Canada's monograph, which sets doses and cautions. It is not for everyone: the monograph cautions include pregnancy and breastfeeding, and you should speak to a pharmacist if you take thyroid, sedative or immune-related medicines. Buy a licensed product and read the label.

**Are the herbs sold in Indian grocery stores in Calgary safe?**
Check each one the same way: an NPN on the label, found in the database. Many imported products have no Canadian licence. Spices sold as food (turmeric powder, cumin, fenugreek) are regulated as food, not as natural health products, and are fine to cook with.

**What are the signs of heavy-metal exposure?**
Health Canada lists stomach pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea among early signs, with longer exposure harming the kidneys, nervous system and blood. If you have taken an unlicensed product for a long time and feel unwell, see your physician and mention the product by name; a blood test can check lead levels.

**Does Health Canada test Ayurvedic products before they are sold?**
It reviews the licence application, including ingredients and manufacturing, and inspects licensed sites; it does not test every batch on the shelf. It does test and seize products when a complaint or a poisoning case points to them, as in the Toronto case.

**Can I report a suspicious product?**
Yes. Health Canada takes complaints about health products through its online reporting form, and side effects through the Canada Vigilance Program.
