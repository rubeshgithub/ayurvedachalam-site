# Is Ayurveda Regulated in Canada?

*By Maheswari Vedhachalam, BAMS (Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery) · Ayurveda Practitioner, Calgary · Reviewed [date set in CMS]*

No. Ayurveda is legal to practise in Canada, but it is not a regulated health profession in any province or territory. That means there is no Canadian licence, no college and no public register for Ayurvedic practitioners, so the checking falls to you. This article explains what the law does and does not cover, what rules still apply to an Ayurvedic practice in Alberta, and the five things worth checking before you book with anyone, including us.

## What "regulated" means in Canada

In Canada, health professions are regulated by the provinces, not by Ottawa. A regulated profession has a law behind it, a college that keeps a register, entry standards, a complaints process and protected titles. Physicians, nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists and chiropractors are regulated everywhere. Some complementary professions are regulated in some provinces: naturopathic doctors and acupuncturists are regulated in Alberta, and Ontario regulates traditional Chinese medicine and homeopathy.

Ayurveda is on none of these lists. Alberta's register of regulated health professions names 32 professions, and Ayurveda is not among them. Ontario's list of 27 regulated professions does not include it either, even though that list runs to homeopathy and kinesiology. The same is true in every other province.

"Unregulated" is not the same as "illegal". Anyone may offer Ayurvedic consultations and therapies in Canada, as long as they stay within the laws that apply to everyone. Those laws are the real subject of this article.

## Can an Ayurvedic practitioner call themselves a doctor in Canada?

Not in connection with providing a health service, at least not in Alberta. Section 128 of Alberta's *Health Professions Act* protects the title "doctor" and the abbreviation "Dr." when used alone or with other words in connection with providing a health service. Only practitioners authorised under that Act or another law may use it. The Act does not restrict the word in academic or social settings, but a clinic sign, a website or an appointment reminder is a health-service setting.

This matters for Ayurveda in particular. In India, a graduate of a BAMS degree is registered as an Ayurvedic doctor and uses "Dr." every day. That is the correct usage there. It is not permitted here. So on this website you will see the degree spelled out, Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery, and the title Ayurveda Practitioner, with no "Dr." in front of my name. A practitioner in Alberta who uses "Dr." for Ayurvedic services is either unaware of the rule or ignoring it; either way, it is a fair question to ask them.

## What is a BAMS degree, and does Canada recognise it?

BAMS stands for Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery. It is the standard Ayurvedic qualification in India: a 5½-year university degree followed by a one-year to 1½-year rotating internship, regulated in India by the Ministry of AYUSH and its council for Indian systems of medicine. Mine is from Dr. M.G.R. Medical University in Chennai, completed in 2012, with the internship at the Government General Hospital, Chennai.

Does Canada recognise it? In two different senses, with two different answers.

- **As an academic credential, yes.** Credential services such as World Education Services (WES) assess a BAMS and report its Canadian equivalence, which is what immigration and employers use. Mine has been evaluated by WES Canada.
- **As a licence to practise medicine, no.** A BAMS does not make anyone a physician in Canada, and no province licenses Ayurvedic practitioners. I am not a medical doctor here, and nothing on this site should be read that way.

When you are comparing practitioners, the useful question is not "are they licensed?" (nobody is) but "what did they study, for how long, and can they show it?"

## What rules does an Ayurvedic practice in Alberta still have to follow?

Several, and they are the ones that protect you.

**Restricted activities.** Alberta's *Health Professions Act* reserves certain procedures for regulated professionals. Among them are inserting anything beyond the anal verge and beyond the point where the nasal passages narrow. Classical Ayurveda includes internal procedures such as Vasti (enema) and clinic-administered Nasya that fall on the wrong side of that line. An Alberta practice that offers them is offering a restricted activity. That is why the therapies on this site are external: warm-oil pooling (Kati Vasti, Janu Vasti, Greeva Vasti), Shirodhara, Abhyanga and related work, and why our Nasya page is guidance for home use, not a clinic procedure.

**Health claims.** Section 3 of Canada's *Food and Drugs Act* prohibits advertising any food, drug or device to the public as a treatment, preventative or cure for the diseases listed in Schedule A.1, a list that includes cancer, diabetes, arthritis, asthma, depression and many others. Alberta's rules on misleading advertising add to that. A practitioner who promises to "cure" or "reverse" a named disease is making a claim the law does not allow, and I would treat it as a warning sign rather than a selling point.

**Herbal products.** Every natural health product sold in Canada needs a Health Canada product licence before it can be marketed, shown by an eight-digit Natural Product Number (NPN) on the label. Companies that make, package, label or import these products also need a site licence. Health Canada has warned repeatedly about unlicensed Ayurvedic products: a 2008 advisory on heavy metals in unapproved Ayurvedic preparations, and a 2021–22 warning after products seized from a Toronto Ayurvedic clinic were found to contain high levels of lead, mercury or arsenic in 14 of 15 samples, and prescription drugs in three. This practice recommends only products with an NPN and never metal- or mineral-based preparations, however traditional they are.

**Massage therapy.** Massage therapy is regulated in British Columbia, Ontario, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Prince Edward Island. In Alberta it is not yet; the profession's associations have applied to the province, and the government was reviewing that application in January 2025. Until then, standards come from the professional associations, and insurers decide whose receipts they accept. Ayurvedic massage offered by someone who is also a Registered Massage Therapist is held to that association's standards; Ayurvedic massage offered by someone who is not is held to no standard at all.

**Privacy.** A private clinic in Alberta that collects your personal information is covered by the province's *Personal Information Protection Act*. You are entitled to know what is collected, why, and how to see it.

## How can I check an Ayurvedic practitioner before I book?

Since no register exists, do five minutes of your own checking:

1. **Ask for the degree, not the title.** A BAMS, an MD (Ayurveda) or an equivalent, from a named university, with a year. Diploma and certificate courses of a few weeks exist too; they are not the same thing, and a good practitioner will tell you which they hold.
2. **Look for "Dr." where it should not be.** If an Alberta practitioner uses it for Ayurvedic services, ask why.
3. **Read the claims.** "Supports", "traditionally used for" and "many clients find" are honest. "Cures", "reverses" and "guaranteed" are not allowed and not honest.
4. **Ask what is in the bottle.** Any product recommended to you should have an NPN. If it was brought from abroad, has no number, or is a bhasma or rasa preparation, decline it.
5. **Ask what they will not do.** A practitioner who can tell you plainly what is out of bounds here, and why, understands the system they are working in.

## Does this mean Ayurveda in Canada is unsafe?

It means the safeguards are on you and on the practitioner's own standards, not on a college. Consultations about diet, daily routine, seasonal routine, sleep and stress, and external oil therapies given by a trained person, carry low risk and are the core of what Ayurveda offers outside India. The documented harms in Canada have come from unlicensed products containing heavy metals, not from the therapies themselves. Choose the practitioner carefully, keep your physician informed about any herbs you take, and the picture is a reassuring one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Ayurveda legal in Canada?**
Yes. It is legal to practise and to receive. It is simply not a regulated profession, so there is no provincial licence or register for practitioners.

**Can I claim an Ayurvedic consultation on my extended health benefits?**
Usually not. Most plans cover massage therapy by a Registered Massage Therapist, and some cover naturopathy or acupuncture, which are regulated. Ayurvedic consultations and spiritual healing are generally not covered. Check your own plan; our pricing page sets out what we can and cannot receipt.

**Is a BAMS the same as an MD?**
No. A BAMS is a university degree in Ayurvedic medicine from India. It is not a licence to practise medicine in Canada and does not make the holder a physician here.

**Does Ontario or British Columbia regulate Ayurveda?**
No. Ontario regulates 27 health professions, including traditional Chinese medicine, naturopathy and homeopathy, but not Ayurveda. British Columbia regulates massage therapy, naturopathy and traditional Chinese medicine, and not Ayurveda.

**Will Ayurveda become regulated in Canada?**
There is no application before any provincial government that we know of. Regulation usually follows years of work by a profession's own associations. If that changes, this page will be updated.
