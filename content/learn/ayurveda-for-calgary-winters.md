# Ayurveda for Calgary Winters

*By Maheswari Vedhachalam, BAMS (Bachelor of Ayurveda Medicine and Surgery) · Ayurveda Practitioner, Calgary · Reviewed [date set in CMS]*

Calgary's winter is not the damp, grey winter most Ayurvedic winter advice was written for. It is cold, very dry, unusually sunny, and interrupted every few weeks by a chinook that can lift the temperature by twenty degrees in an afternoon. In Ayurvedic terms that is a Vata season through and through: dry, cold, light, mobile and irregular. This article sets out a daily routine for exactly this climate, with foods you can buy at Superstore or the Calgary Farmers' Market, and the two therapies that suit the season best.

## Why Calgary winters are a Vata season

A few numbers from Environment and Climate Change Canada's 1991–2020 climate normals for Calgary International Airport explain a lot. The January daily average is about −7.6 °C. Relative humidity in the afternoon sits near 55 per cent, but at those temperatures the air holds very little moisture, and once it is heated indoors it is drier still. The city receives roughly 2,400 hours of bright sunshine a year, among the most of any large Canadian city, so winter here is bright and high-altitude rather than overcast. And the chinook, the warm, dry wind off the Rockies, can raise the temperature by 20 °C or more within hours, then let it fall back.

Ayurveda describes the world in qualities. Dry, cold, light, rough, mobile and irregular are the qualities of Vata, and a Calgary winter has every one of them: dry air, cold, wind, and a temperature that will not sit still. When the season's qualities pile up in a person, the tradition expects dry skin and lips, static in the hair, cracked heels, constipation, light or broken sleep, cold hands and feet, creaking joints, and a mind that runs faster than it needs to. Chinook days add their own pattern: many Calgarians report headaches, poor sleep and restlessness when the wind arrives. Those are commonly reported experiences; whether chinooks cause headaches is a question researchers still argue about.

The Ayurvedic answer is not complicated. You counter dry with oil, cold with warmth, light with nourishing food, and irregular with routine. Here is what that looks like between November and March.

## Morning: oil before the shower

**Abhyanga at home.** Warm two or three tablespoons of sesame oil (plain, cold-pressed, from the grocery store; not toasted) by standing the bottle in a mug of hot water. Before your shower, massage it over the whole body for five to ten minutes: long strokes on the limbs, circles on the joints and belly, and a minute on the soles of the feet. Leave it on for ten minutes if you can, then shower warm, not hot. Use soap only where you need it. In a Calgary January this one habit does more for skin than any cream, because the oil is under the water rather than on top of it. Put a towel down; oil and bathroom floors do not mix.

**Nasal oil.** Dry heated air dries the nasal passages first. A drop of plain sesame oil or ghee on a clean fingertip, rubbed just inside each nostril after your shower, is the home form of Nasya and is safe for most adults. We do not perform Nasya at the clinic; this is a home habit, and if you have nosebleeds, sinus surgery or a nasal condition, ask your physician before starting.

**Warm water, then a warm breakfast.** A cup of warm water on waking. Then porridge rather than cold cereal: oats or cream of wheat cooked with a little ghee or butter, cinnamon, cardamom, stewed apple or soaked raisins. Cold smoothies and yogurt straight from the fridge are summer food here.

## Day: warmth, weight and routine

**Lunch is the main meal.** Digestion is strongest in the middle of the day. Soups, dals, stews, root vegetables, rice, well-cooked grains; warm, moist, a little oily, gently spiced with cumin, ginger, black pepper, fennel. Alberta's winter produce is on your side: potatoes, carrots, beets, squash, parsnips, onions. Salads can wait for May.

**Sip warm water through the day.** Dry air pulls water out of you without your noticing it. A thermos of warm water with a slice of fresh ginger, carried everywhere, is the cheapest piece of Ayurvedic advice there is.

**Cover the head and ears outside.** Vata enters through wind on the head. A toque is Ayurveda.

**Keep the times steady.** Wake, eat and sleep at roughly the same times, including weekends. Of all the winter advice, this is the one clients find hardest and report the most from. Irregularity is the quality the season already has in excess; your routine is what you can control.

**On chinook days,** expect to feel scattered and plan for it: a lighter schedule if you can, warm food, less caffeine, oil on the scalp or soles in the evening, and an early night. Do not treat the warm afternoon as spring; the dryness is at its worst on those days.

## Evening: wind down early

**A warm, early, light supper** by seven: soup, kitchari (rice and split mung beans cooked soft with ghee and spices), or leftover lunch. **Warm milk** before bed, if you take dairy, with a pinch of nutmeg or turmeric and a little ghee, is the tradition's sleep aid; oat milk warmed the same way works for those who do not. **Oil the soles of the feet** before sleep, and the scalp once or twice a week. **Screens off** an hour before bed; the light is Vata too. **In bed by ten**, which in a Calgary December, when it is dark by five, is easier than it sounds.

## Who should take care with this routine

Oil massage and warm food are safe for almost everyone. A few cautions: skip self-massage over broken skin, rashes or varicose veins, and during a fever; do not use a hot shower after oil if you have very low blood pressure and feel faint; if you have diabetes, check the soles of your feet when you oil them and tell us before any foot therapy; use ghee and whole milk in the quantities you would normally eat if you are watching cholesterol or weight, and ask your physician or dietitian if unsure; and if dry skin cracks, bleeds or does not improve, see your physician, since persistent skin changes can have medical causes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which oil should I buy for self-massage in Calgary?**
Plain cold-pressed sesame oil from the grocery store or an Indian grocer; one litre lasts a season. If sesame is too warming or you have a sesame allergy, sunflower or almond oil. Avoid toasted sesame oil, which is for cooking.

**What about a humidifier?**
Yes. Ayurveda would approve; it adds the quality the air lacks. Keep it clean, and aim for the humidity level Health Canada recommends for homes in winter.

**Is this routine suitable for children?**
Warm oil massage before a bath is traditional for children and well loved. Food and sleep advice applies in a gentler form. Nasal oil and herbs are not for children without specific advice.

**I feel worse on chinook days. Is that real?**
Many Calgarians report it, and clinics are busier for headaches when the wind comes. Research on the cause is mixed. Whatever the mechanism, the Vata routine above is what Ayurveda offers for it, and clients commonly say it helps them ride the day out.

**Does Ayurveda say anything about winter sun?**
Yes: use it. Calgary's bright winter is a gift compared with most of the country. A short walk at midday, face to the sun and head covered, is in the tradition's daily routine and in most physicians' advice too.
